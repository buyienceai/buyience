 A B2B storefront is judged on one thing: whether a buyer can trust what it shows enough to act without phoning you. That means their own negotiated price, stock they can actually get, the payment terms they already have, a fast path to repeat what they bought last time, and a clean handoff to a person when the order needs judgment. Polish matters far less than whether the storefront tells the same story your sales team does. If you are deciding how to roll self-service out to your accounts, start with our guide to [moving routine orders from phone and email to self-service](/blog/industrial-supply-distributors-moving-from-phone-email-orders-to-self-service). This post covers what the storefront itself has to get right.

## Why a B2B storefront is not a smaller B2C shop

Consumer ecommerce is built for discovery: a stranger arrives, browses, compares and pays by card. A distributor's buyer is rarely a stranger. They already have an account, an agreed price, a credit line and usually a list of things they buy every month. They are not shopping. They are trying to get work done.

That changes what "good" means. A B2C store wins on persuasion. A B2B storefront wins on accuracy and speed, and it loses the moment it shows something that contradicts what the buyer was told.

Buyers increasingly want to do this work alone. Gartner's sales survey published in June 2025 found that 61% of B2B buyers prefer an overall rep-free buying experience, and a follow-up survey released in March 2026 put the figure at 67%. Those are surveys of B2B buyers across industries rather than distributors specifically, so treat them as direction, not as a forecast for your own accounts.

The same June 2025 survey contains the finding that matters more for storefront design: 69% of buyers reported inconsistencies between what a supplier's website said and what its sellers told them. A storefront that disagrees with your reps does not just fail to help. It teaches the buyer that the online channel cannot be trusted, and sends them back to the phone with less confidence than before.

There is a second tension worth knowing about. Gartner reported back in 2021 that buyers who prefer a rep-free experience had 23% higher purchase regret than buyers who involved a sales rep. Buyers want to go it alone, and going it alone goes wrong more often. A good storefront is designed around both facts: it removes the rep from orders that do not need one, and makes it easy to bring a person in for the ones that do.

The rest of this post is organised around the five questions a buyer is actually asking when they log in.

## "Is this my price?"

This is the first thing a logged-in buyer checks, and the one that breaks trust fastest. If the storefront shows a list price, a generic "trade" price, or a price that differs from their last invoice, the buyer will either call to confirm or stop using the channel.

What the storefront needs:

**Prices resolved per account, not per catalogue.** The buyer should see the price produced by their customer group, their tier and any contract terms, without having to ask. This depends on [customer-specific pricing](/blog/how-b2b-teams-manage-customer-specific-pricing-at-scale) being managed in one place.

**Volume breaks shown before the cart.** If ordering 500 instead of 400 drops the unit price, the buyer needs to see that on the product page, not discover it at checkout.

**One source of truth.** The price a rep quotes by phone and the price on the storefront must come from the same rules. If they live in two systems, they will drift, and you are back to the 69% problem.

The test is simple: pick three accounts with different price agreements, log in as each, and check whether the prices match what your sales team would quote today.

## "Can I actually get it, and when?"

A B2B buyer is usually ordering against a job, a production run or a customer commitment. "In stock" without context is not enough, and stock that turns out to be wrong is worse than no stock information at all.

What the storefront needs:

**Availability the buyer can rely on.** If you run more than one location, the number shown should reflect stock that is genuinely available, not stock already promised to someone else. That requires [multi-warehouse inventory](/blog/multi-warehouse-inventory-for-industrial-suppliers-a-practical-guide) where stock is reserved when an order is placed.

**An honest signal when stock is short.** A buyer who needs 1,000 units and sees 600 should know that before checking out, along with what happens next: backorder, partial shipment, or a conversation.

**No stale catalogue numbers.** A nightly export from your ERP will be wrong by mid-morning on a busy day. If live data is not possible, say how current the figure is.

McKinsey's B2B Pulse research lists showing product availability online among the capabilities buyers most want from suppliers. It is not a nice-to-have.

## "Will this go through on our terms?"

Checkout is where B2B and B2C diverge most. Many business buyers do not pay by card at all. They buy on account, against a credit limit, and often need internal sign-off before an order can be placed.

What the storefront needs:

**Their payment terms at checkout.** A buyer with [Net 30/60/90 terms](/blog/net-30-60-90-terms-in-industrial-distribution-what-actually-works) should be able to place an order on account, without entering a card or calling accounts.

**Their currency.** If you sell across borders, the buyer should see and pay in the currency they were quoted in.

**A way to match their purchasing process.** Larger customers often need a PO reference, a requisition approval or a spend limit per user. Ask your biggest accounts what their purchasing team requires before you assume a simple checkout will do.

That last point is where many SMB-focused platforms, including ours, are still thin. Be clear with yourself about which of your customers need buyer-side approval workflows, and check whether a platform actually supports them before committing.

## "I've ordered this before"

If your accounts buy the same parts regularly, much of their storefront use will be repeating something they have bought before. The buyer knows the part number, roughly the quantity and the price they expect to pay. Anything that makes them search for it again is friction.

At minimum, the buyer needs their order history in front of them and a way to repeat a past order or reorder individual lines from it, at today's price for their account. Bulk entry by SKU and saved lists help buyers who order dozens of lines at once.

We cover this in depth in our post on [why the B2B reorder workflow breaks](/blog/b2b-reordering-is-broken-and-why-that-matters), so we will not repeat it here. The storefront-level point is that reordering should be the shortest path on the site, not buried under the catalogue.

## "I need to talk to someone"

This is the question most storefront projects forget. Not every order is routine. A first order from a new site, an unusual quantity, a substitution, or a price that needs negotiating all benefit from a person. Gartner's 2025 survey found that buyers still prefer seller input for tasks that need contextual judgment, such as deciding whether a product fits their needs.

What the storefront needs:

**A visible route from product to quote.** When the listed price or quantity does not fit, the buyer should be able to ask for a quote from the page they are on, with the items they were looking at already attached.

**Continuity.** The rep who picks up the request should see what the buyer was viewing and their account history, not start from scratch.

**A shared place to agree the deal.** Negotiation that drifts across email threads loses detail. A [digital sales room](/blog/digital-sales-room-dsr) keeps the quote, the revisions and the conversation in one record that both sides can see.

The goal is not to push every buyer to self-service. It is to make self-service the easy path for routine orders, and make the handoff to a person easy for everything else.

## How to audit your own storefront

You can run this check in an afternoon, without buying anything:

| Buyer question | Test | Pass looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is this my price? | Log in as three accounts with different agreements | Prices match what a rep would quote today |
| Can I get it? | Compare storefront stock to the warehouse for five SKUs | Numbers match, and stock already promised is not shown as available |
| Will it go through on our terms? | Place a test order as an on-account customer | Order completes on their terms, in their currency |
| I've ordered this before | Repeat a past order with ten or more lines | Done in a few clicks, at current account prices |
| I need someone | Request a quote from a product page | Request reaches a person with the items and account attached |

Any row that fails is a reason buyers are still calling you.

## FAQ

### What is a B2B storefront?

A B2B storefront is an online ordering site for business customers. Unlike a consumer shop, it shows each logged-in buyer their own negotiated prices, available stock and payment terms, and lets them place orders on account, repeat past orders or request a quote.

### How is a B2B storefront different from a B2C ecommerce site?

A B2C site is built for discovery and card payment by anonymous shoppers. A B2B storefront is built for known accounts with agreed prices, credit terms and repeat orders. Accuracy of price and stock matters more than persuasion.

### What features should a B2B storefront have?

At minimum: account-specific pricing with visible volume breaks, reliable stock availability, checkout on the buyer's payment terms, order history with reordering, and a way to request a quote or reach a person from the product page.

### Do B2B buyers really want self-service?

Gartner surveys found 61% of B2B buyers preferred a rep-free experience in 2025 and 67% in 2026. Gartner also reported that buyers who preferred going rep-free had higher purchase regret, so the storefront should make it easy to bring in a person when needed.

### Why do buyers stop using a B2B storefront?

Most often because it shows information they cannot trust: a price that differs from what their rep quoted, or stock that turns out to be unavailable. Once a buyer has to call to check, they stop treating the storefront as a reliable channel.
