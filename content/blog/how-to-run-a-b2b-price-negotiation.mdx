**TL;DR:** In B2B distribution the buyer is usually a trained procurement professional and the seller is a rep who negotiates between other duties. That asymmetry, not weak pricing, is where most margin is lost. The counters are preparation (know your floor before the call), trading non-price variables, and concession discipline — never concede twice without reciprocity, always attach a condition.

A procurement manager negotiates for a living. They have been trained in it, they are measured on it, and they will run the same handful of proven moves on your rep that they ran on your competitor last week.

Your rep negotiates occasionally, between servicing accounts and chasing orders, usually without training and often without knowing their own floor until after the deal is done.

That asymmetry is where most distribution margin goes. Not to bad pricing structures — to competent people being outplayed in a conversation the other side had prepared for.

## Prepare three numbers before the conversation

Walking into a negotiation knowing only your list price is walking in with one number against someone holding several.

**Your floor.** The price below which this deal stops being worth doing. Not a feeling — the actual number, calculated from landed cost. If a rep doesn't know it before the call, they will discover it afterwards, which is too late to act on.

**Your opening.** Where you start, with enough room to concede without crossing the floor. If your opening is already near your floor, you have no negotiation to run — you have an announcement, and any pressure at all pushes you under.

**Your walk-away.** Distinct from the floor. The floor is about this transaction; the walk-away is about the relationship. Some accounts are worth a thin deal to keep. Some aren't, and continuing to serve them at a loss is a decision you should be making deliberately rather than by default.

Alongside the numbers, the history: what this customer usually pays, how often they push, whether they have ever walked. A rep who can see the last twelve months of [this account's pricing history](/blog/how-b2b-teams-manage-customer-specific-pricing-at-scale) is negotiating from a different position than one who is guessing.

## Trade something other than price

The most common error is treating a negotiation as a single-variable problem. The buyer says the price is too high; the rep's only lever is the price.

In distribution there are usually several variables in play, and most cost you less than margin does:

**Payment terms.** Moving from Net 60 to Net 30, or to payment on delivery, has real value to you and is often easier for a buyer to concede than price, because it comes out of a different budget line. That's what makes [payment terms as a lever](/blog/net-30-60-90-terms-in-industrial-distribution-what-actually-works) so effective.

**Volume commitment.** A better unit price in exchange for a committed annual volume or a minimum order quantity. Structured properly, [volume commitments](/blog/volume-discount-structures-that-actually-protect-your-margin) convert a discount into something you get paid for.

**Order consolidation.** Fewer, larger orders cut your handling and freight cost per unit. Price against the consolidated pattern, not the fragmented one.

**Lead time.** A buyer who can accept a longer lead time lets you order more efficiently. One who needs it faster should pay for the expedite rather than receiving it free alongside a discount.

**Delivery scheduling.** Staged delivery across a quarter helps your inventory planning and is frequently neutral to the buyer.

**Product mix.** A thinner margin on the competitive line, held firm on the ancillary items that go with it.

**Packaging and handling.** Bulk packing instead of individually packed units is a genuine cost difference.

The principle: every concession should buy something. A discount given for nothing teaches the buyer that asking is sufficient, and they will ask again on every subsequent order.

## Concession discipline

How you concede matters more than how much.

**Decreasing increments.** If your first move is 4% and your second is 3%, the buyer reads an infinite ladder and keeps climbing. Go 4%, then 1.5%, then 0.5% and the pattern says you are approaching a limit. The shape of the sequence communicates more than any statement that this is your final offer.

**Never two in a row.** If you concede and the buyer simply asks again without moving, do not concede again. Restate, and ask what moves on their side. Two unreciprocated concessions establish that pushing works.

**Always attach a condition.** "I can do that price with Net 30 instead of Net 60" rather than "fine, I can do that price." The conditional form keeps the trade visible and gives you something to withdraw if they decline.

**Slow down.** An instant concession signals there was margin in the original number — and that there is probably more. Taking time to check, even briefly, is itself information to the buyer.

## Handling "your competitor is cheaper"

This is the most-used move in distribution procurement, and often the least examined.

**First, establish that it's comparable.** Same specification, same quantity, same lead time, same terms, same freight treatment. Frequently it isn't, and asking politely for the detail resolves a meaningful share of these without any price movement at all.

**If it is genuinely comparable and genuinely cheaper, you have a real decision.** Matching it trains the buyer to bring you a competitor quote every cycle. Not matching may cost the order. The deciding question is what the account is worth at the matched price — which is the floor and walk-away calculation you did beforehand.

**Consider matching conditionally rather than outright.** Matching on this order in exchange for a volume commitment, or matching on this line while holding the rest of the basket, concedes less than a straight match and keeps the pattern from setting.

**Be willing to lose some.** A distributor who never loses on price is leaving margin on the table everywhere else. Losing an occasional deal to a competitor pricing below your floor is a correct outcome, not a failure.

## What the revision count tells you

Multi-round negotiations carry information that a single quote doesn't.

A buyer who comes back quickly with a specific counter is engaged and probably close. One who takes two weeks and returns with a vague "can you do better" is often shopping your number elsewhere and using it as leverage.

Rounds three and four are where discipline usually breaks. The rep has invested time, the end of the month is approaching, and the sunk cost makes a thin deal feel better than no deal. That is precisely when the floor matters — it exists to be the constraint in exactly this moment, when judgement is least reliable.

## Capture what happened

Most distributors lose negotiations twice: once at the table, and again by not recording why.

What's worth capturing: the price agreed, what was traded for it, who approved any exception, and the reason. That record does three jobs — it answers the customer's question next quarter about why the price moved, it shows whether a particular account is ratcheting you down over successive orders, and it lets a new rep inheriting the account see the shape of the relationship rather than starting cold.

Without it, every negotiation starts from scratch and the buyer, who does keep records, holds a permanent advantage.

## FAQ: B2B Price Negotiation for Distributors

### What should a rep know before a price negotiation starts?

Three numbers — the floor below which the deal is not worth doing, an opening with room to concede above it, and a walk-away point for the relationship rather than the transaction. Plus the account's pricing history, so the rep is negotiating from record rather than memory.

### What can I trade instead of discounting?

Payment terms, volume or minimum-order commitments, order consolidation, longer lead times, staged delivery scheduling, product mix, and packaging or handling arrangements. Most cost less than margin, and several are easier for a buyer to concede because they come from a different budget.

### How should concessions be structured?

In decreasing increments, so the pattern signals a limit. Never concede twice without the buyer moving in return, and always attach a condition — "I can do that with Net 30" rather than an unconditional yes, so the trade stays visible.

### How do I handle "your competitor is cheaper"?

Establish comparability first on specification, quantity, lead time, terms and freight, which resolves many cases without movement. If genuinely comparable and cheaper, decide against your floor and walk-away rather than matching reflexively, and prefer a conditional match to an outright one.

### When is it right to lose a deal on price?

When the price required is below your floor. A distributor who never loses on price is almost certainly conceding too readily elsewhere, and losing to a competitor pricing below your cost is a correct outcome rather than a failure.

### Why does recording negotiation outcomes matter?

It answers the customer's question next quarter about why their price changed, reveals whether an account is ratcheting you down across successive orders, and gives a rep inheriting the account the relationship's history. Buyers keep records; sellers who don't negotiate at a permanent disadvantage.
